# Writing the policy, step by step

Eight steps. Tell the user the step number, the purpose, and what done looks like. Keep this order even if a shortcut seems possible: each step builds on the one before, and the checkpoints are where leaders own the result. Sitting one covers steps 1 to 4; sitting two covers 5 to 7; step 8 happens among the leaders. Update `HANDOFF.md` at the end of each sitting.

Invite the user to work the current way: "Tell me the outcome you want and give me your documents; I'll propose a plan and ask questions. Push back when something doesn't sound like you."

## Demonstration

Use [the Cedar Hill sample](../assets/demo-cedar-hill-inputs.md) as if it were a leader's answers and run steps 2 to 7 in short form: the promise, five principles with their anchors and status, a question filter tested on the four sample tasks, the human-only and reviewed categories, a two-page draft and the quick guide. Label everything "Fictional sample data". About 15 minutes. The sample's closing list says what a careful run should catch.

## Step 1 of 8: setup

Run [onboarding](onboarding.md).
Done: profile and `HANDOFF.md` saved, source documents read. Why: every later step draws on these documents, so a missing one shows up now, not in the draft.

## Step 2 of 8: convictions and promise

Ask what the ministry believes about work, people, truth and technology, drawing first on their own documents. Ask how their tradition speaks about these things; do not supply a tradition's view for them. Draft a short promise: 3 to 5 sentences on what AI could free the ministry for. Read it back.
Done: the user says "that sounds like us". Why: every later rule is tested against this, so it has to be theirs.

## Step 3 of 8: principles and Scripture anchors

Draft 4 to 6 principles from their convictions, each a posture in one or two sentences. For each, ask which passage anchors it for them. If they ask for suggestions, offer two or three real references per principle, marked "suggested", and tell them to read each in context. Record each anchor as proposed or approved.
Done: the pastor or teaching leader has approved every anchor; any still unapproved stays marked and goes on the open-items list. Why: a misapplied verse in a policy teaches the congregation something false.

## Step 4 of 8: the question filter

Build 3 to 5 questions anyone can ask before using AI for a task. Draw them from the principles, in the ministry's own words, answerable yes or no. Test them together on three real tasks the user names (one should clearly fail).
Done: the filter separates the tasks the way the leaders would. Why: a filter that passes everything protects no one.

## Step 5 of 8: where people must stay involved

Use the outline's review rules. Ask which tasks are human-only, with AI allowed only for preparation or follow-up: pastoral care, counseling and crisis conversations; decisions about people (hiring, discipline, benevolence, membership); and any others they name. Ask what must be reviewed by a named person before use: anything published or sent in the ministry's name, teaching content, anything touching confidential data. Then cover AI tools that act on their own (send, schedule, spend, change records): help the ministry write its own levels and conditions, using the outline's questions, and record who owns each tool and how to stop it.
Done: every category has a rule and a responsible role. Why: a tool that acts can make a mistake faster than anyone notices, and the ministry, not the tool's maker, answers for it.

## Step 6 of 8: staff and volunteer guidelines, privacy, disclosure

Draft plain do and don't lists. Map the ministry's data into categories and say which tools may see each, building on the sensitivity answer. Ask when AI help must be disclosed, and whether AI-made voices or likenesses of real people are allowed. Add the protecting-our-people section if they want it.
Done: a staff member could read the lists and know what to do Monday. Why: most real incidents come from a well-meaning person who had no rule to follow.

## Step 7 of 8: assemble, check and review

Assemble the policy with [policy outline](policy-outline.md): 2 to 4 pages, labeled "Version 0.1 DRAFT". Then the one-page staff quick guide. Save files with a `-Claude` suffix (for example `ai-policy-v0.1-Claude.docx`) so everyone can see what Claude drafted.
Before people review, run a second-model check: in a fresh chat, or with a different model if the user's plan offers one, give it the draft and the source documents and ask: "Audit this policy against our own documents and the anchor list; grade each section; fix anything below an A and list what you changed." It should catch any unapproved or inaccurate anchor, anything invented, any contradiction with their documents, and anything too vague to act on.
Then a structured review round: offer a review sheet (a spreadsheet or table with columns: section, text, decision keep/change/cut, comment, reviewer). The drafting team fills it in; you work in every comment and report what changed. Repeat until the team is ready. If the user's plan includes artifacts, offer an interactive review page for the team.
Done: the drafting team agrees it is ready for the approving body. Why: a fresh reader catches what the writer has stopped seeing, and comments in one sheet get answered where comments in five emails get lost.

## Step 8 of 8: approval and versions

The approving body reads, amends and adopts it. Claude does not present it to them. After adoption, the user tells you the date: mark it "Version 1.0, approved [date] by [body]", remove DRAFT, update the profile, and set the next review date. Offer to set up a yearly reminder or scheduled task if their setup supports it. A person decides whether to publish it (for example as a web page or handout for teaching) and does the publishing.
Done: version 1.0 is marked approved, the profile is updated and the next review date is set. Why: the policy carries weight only because the body chose it.

## Revising or yearly review

Load the profile and the current version. Ask what changed: new tools, an incident, new teaching or research, staff feedback. Current models revise best with the whole current policy, the new material, and a bounded request. Suggest: "Propose about 10 to 12 targeted changes, each with a reason and the section it touches. Keep the policy no more than 10 to 15 percent longer." Show the change list first; apply only the approved changes.
Check how current each outside source is. AI changes fast: a source that tested an old model, or describes tools that no longer work that way, may be out of date. Say so and let the leaders decide whether to use it.
Version numbers: small wording fixes 1.1, 1.2; approved substantive revisions 2.0. Keep a version log in the policy. Every new version goes back through step 7's checks and step 8's approval.

## Useful follow-up prompts

- "Test our question filter on these five tasks and show where it's unclear."
- "Show every Scripture anchor with its status and the principle it supports."
- "Audit this draft against our statement of faith and staff handbook; grade each section and fix anything below an A."
- "Turn the review sheet comments into a revised draft and list what changed."
- "Here are new notes on AI and ministry. Propose up to 12 changes, no more than 10 percent longer."
- "Make the staff quick guide fit on one printed page."
